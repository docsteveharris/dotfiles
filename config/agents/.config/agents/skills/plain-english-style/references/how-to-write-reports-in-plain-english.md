# How to write reports in plain English

Source: Plain English Campaign — https://www.plainenglish.co.uk/free-guides

## Introduction

There is no great mystery about writing clear, concise and effective reports. The writing skills in this guide work in all types of business writing — letters, leaflets, memos and so on. What makes reports different is the formal way they are organised.

## So what's plain English?

- It's not 'cat sat on the mat' or 'Peter and Jane' writing. Almost anything can be written in plain English without being patronising or over-simple.
- It doesn't mean reducing the length or changing the meaning of your message.
- It's not about banning new words, killing off long words or promoting completely perfect grammar.
- It is not an amateur's method of communication. Most forward-looking senior managers always write in plain English.
- It is not as easy as we would like to think.

The advantages of plain English are:

- it is faster to write;
- it is faster to read; and
- you get your message across more often, more easily and in a friendlier way.

## Keep your sentences short

Most experts agree that clear writing should have an average sentence length of 15–20 words. This does not mean making every sentence the same length. Be punchy. Vary your writing by mixing short sentences with longer ones, following the basic principle of sticking to one main idea in a sentence, plus perhaps one other related point.

## Actives and passives

Passive verbs make writing duller and harder to understand. Active verbs make writing livelier and more personal.

In active sentences, the doer comes first, then the verb, then the thing: "The boss slammed the door." With passive verbs, the thing comes first: "The door was slammed by the boss." This introduces 'was' and 'by', making the sentence clumsier.

Sentences with passive verbs can make sense without a doer at all — e.g. "the door was slammed", "the cheque had been cashed", "the report is being written". Officialese is full of these:

- 35 sites were visited in three weeks. Procedures were being properly followed at the sites visited.
- Overheads were not kept under control despite an awareness of the budgetary situation.

Neither sentence has a doer, so the reader is left asking "who visited the sites?" Active versions reveal the doer:

- We visited 35 sites in three weeks. At the sites we visited, we found that staff were following procedures properly.
- Managers were not keeping overheads under control, despite knowing about the budgetary situation.

### Spotting passives

Passive verbs almost always include a form of 'to be' (be, being, am, are, is, was, were, will be) plus a past participle (asked, claimed, done, written).

Examples: "Care should be taken…", "The outcome will be decided…", "Applicants will only be accepted if…"

### Good uses of passives

- To make something less hostile: "this bill has not been paid" (passive) is softer than "you have not paid this bill" (active).
- To avoid taking the blame: "a mistake was made" (passive) rather than "we made a mistake" (active).
- When you don't know who or what the doer is: "the England team has been picked".
- If it simply sounds better.

Aim to make about 80–90% of your verbs active.

## Talk to your reader

Write with your reader in mind. Imagine you are presenting your report to your reader yourself. Think carefully about what they already know and what you need to tell them.

### 'I' and 'we'

Reports used to be full of passive verbs to let the writer remain anonymous, using phrases like "it was found that", "it is accepted that", "it is recommended that". But usually everyone knows who wrote the report. Let readers know there is a person behind the print — use "we found that", "I accept that", "we recommend that".

## Understandable words

Say exactly what you mean, using the simplest words that fit. Don't use jargon that is part of your working life unless writing to someone who uses the same jargon. In general, use everyday English whenever you can. Write to communicate, not to impress.

## What is a nominalisation?

A 'nominalisation' is a type of abstract noun — the name of a process, technique or emotion rather than a physical object, formed from a verb.

| Verb | Nominalisation |
|---|---|
| complete | completion |
| introduce | introduction |
| provide | provision |
| fail | failure |
| arrange | arrangement |
| investigate | investigation |
| use | utilisation |

Writers often use nominalisations when they should use the verb they come from. Like passive verbs, too many nominalisations make writing dull and heavy-going.

Examples:

- We had a discussion about the matter. → We discussed the matter.
- The report made reference to staff shortages. → The report referred to staff shortages.
- The decision was taken by the Board. → The Board decided.
- The implementation of the policy has been done by a team. → A team has implemented the policy.

## Cutting out useless words

Combine all techniques: shorten sentences, use active verbs, use everyday English, personalise, replace nominalisations, and cut out useless words.

## Other points to consider

### Sounding positive

Always try to emphasise the positive side of things.

- Negative: If you don't send your payment, we won't be able to renew your membership of the scheme.
- Positive: Please send your payment so that we can renew your membership of the scheme.

## Using lists

Lists are excellent for splitting information up. There are two main types:

- A continuous sentence with several listed points picked out at the beginning, middle or end.
- A list of separate points with an introductory statement.

For short separate points, set out like this:

Kevin needed to take:
- a penknife
- some string
- a pad of paper
- a pen.

For a list that is part of a continuous sentence, use semicolons after each point and start each with a lower-case letter:

If you can prove that:
- you were somewhere else at the time;
- you were not related to Mary; and
- you are over 21;

then you should be all right.

Use bullet points rather than numbers or letters, as they draw attention to each point without adding extra information to take in.

## Some more myths destroyed

Our rule: if it makes your sentence clearer, don't worry about traditional 'bad form'.

- You can start a sentence with 'and', 'but', 'because', 'so', 'or' or 'however'.
- You can split infinitives — "to boldly go".
- You can end a sentence with a preposition.
- You can repeat a word in a sentence if you can't find a better one.

This doesn't mean breaking these so-called rules all the time — just when it helps a sentence flow better.

## Planning and organising reports

Before writing a report:

- define its purpose carefully;
- investigate the topic thoroughly; and
- organise the information into sections.

### Defining the purpose

This helps you be clear about why you are writing, what to include, what to leave out, and who your readers are. If you can express the purpose in a single sentence, so much the better.

### Investigating the topic

Depends on the topic and purpose — you may need to read, interview, experiment and observe.

### Organising the report into sections

Reports can be set out in eight parts, though you won't always need them all:

- Title or title page
- Contents list
- Abstract
- Introduction
- Discussion
- Summary and conclusions
- Recommendations
- Appendix

A short report won't need a title page but should have a title. The contents list is only needed in long reports. The abstract is only needed in formal reports (e.g. scientific research) — keep it 80–120 words, and don't confuse it with an executive summary.

**Introduction** should be brief and answer:
- What is the topic?
- Who asked for the report and why?
- What is the background?
- What was your method of working? (If long, put in an appendix.)
- What were the sources? (If many, put in an appendix.)

**Discussion** is the main body — usually the longest section. Few readers read every word, so start with the most important point, then the next most important, and so on. Apply the same rule within each paragraph.

**Summary and conclusions** describes the purpose of the report, your conclusions and how you reached them. Keep conclusions brief — what options or actions you consider best, and what can be learned. This may lead to recommendations.

Often summary, conclusions and recommendations are combined and circulated separately as an "executive summary" so readers get the key information without reading the whole report.

**Appendix** is for material readers only need if studying the report in depth. Relevant charts and tables should go in the discussion; only use an appendix if they would disrupt the flow.

### Order of presentation

**Long reports**: Title/title page → (Contents list) → (Abstract) → Introduction → Summary and conclusions → Recommendations → Discussion → (Appendix)

**Short reports**: Title → Introduction → Discussion → Summary and conclusions → Recommendations → (Appendix)

### Order of writing

Introduction → Discussion → Summary and conclusion → Recommendations → (Abstract) → Title or title page → (Contents list) → (Appendix)

Write in this order because each section you finish helps you write the next one. After writing all sections, read, revise and rewrite as necessary.

### Numbering sections and paragraphs

Clear headings and a full contents list are usually enough. If labelling is needed, use capital letters for sections and numbers for paragraphs (A1, A2, etc.), and small letters for parts of paragraphs if necessary.

### Planning the writing

Two methods:

**Outline**: pour out all facts, ideas and observations at random onto paper in note form as fast as possible. Then organise, group and assess them for strength and relevance. Number the points or add headings ('Intro', 'Discussion', 'Conclusion'). Leave it for a day or two, then return with fresh ideas.

**Mind mapping**: put the topic in a box in the middle of the page, then draw branching lines for main ideas. Easy to add new information and links; order often takes care of itself.

### Revision

Always read critically what you've written. Leave it for a few days if possible, then re-read, or ask someone else to read it. Ask: "Is this clear, concise and persuasive?" Be prepared to revise language and structure, even rewrite parts that don't work.

## 'Brevity' — Memo to the War Cabinet, Winston Churchill, 9 August 1940

> To do our work, we all have to read a mass of papers. Nearly all of them are far too long. This wastes time, while energy has to be spent in looking for the essential points. I ask my colleagues and their staff to see to it that their reports are shorter.
>
> The aim should be reports which set out the main points in a series of short, crisp paragraphs. If a report relies on detailed analysis of some complicated factors, or on statistics, these should be set out in an appendix.
>
> Often the occasion is best met by submitting not a full-dress report, but an 'aide-memoire' consisting of headings only, which can be expanded orally if needed.
>
> Let us have an end of such phrases as these: 'It is also of importance to bear in mind the following considerations', or 'Consideration should be given to the possibility of carrying into effect'. Most of these woolly phrases are mere padding, which can be left out altogether, or replaced by a single word. Let us not shrink from using the short expressive phrase, even if it is conversational.
>
> Reports drawn up on the lines I propose may first seem rough as compared with the flat surface of officialese jargon. But the saving in time will be great, while the discipline of setting out the real points concisely will prove an aid to clearer thinking.

## Summary

- Plan carefully before you start writing. Use an outline or a mind map.
- Organise your report into sections.
- Use everyday English whenever possible.
- Avoid jargon and legalistic words, and explain any technical terms you have to use.
- Keep your sentence length down to an average of 15 to 20 words. Stick to one main idea in a sentence.
- Use active verbs as much as possible. Say 'we will do it' rather than 'it will be done by us'.
- Be concise.
- Imagine you are talking to your reader. Write sincerely, personally, in a suitable style and tone.
- Always check that your report is accurate, clear, concise and readable.

## Words to avoid

Try to use the alternatives suggested in brackets:

- additional (extra)
- advise (tell)
- applicant (you)
- commence (start)
- complete (fill in)
- comply with (keep to)
- consequently (so)
- ensure (make sure)
- forward (send)
- in accordance with (under, keeping to)
- in excess of (more than)
- in respect of (for)
- in the event of (if)

- per annum (a year)
- persons (people)
- prior to (before)
- purchase (buy)
- regarding (about)
