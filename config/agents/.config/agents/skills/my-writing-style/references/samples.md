# Voice samples

Selected excerpts for calibrating Steve's writing across registers. These are examples to learn from, not templates to reproduce.

## How to use this file

Read the samples closest to the task. Notice how the argument works, how much explanation the reader needs, and how the relationship shapes the language.

Do not borrow facts, opinions, personal experiences or distinctive phrases for unrelated drafts.

### Provenance and editorial conventions

- Samples 1–5 reproduce excerpts from the correspondence and personal writing supplied by Steve in the earlier samples file. They have not been independently checked against the original documents.
- Samples 6–10 come from the five PDFs supplied by Steve. Sole-authored and co-authored work are distinguished below.
- PDF line wrapping, spacing and line-break hyphenation have been normalised. Published wording is otherwise retained in the selected passages.
- Separate passages are labelled as excerpts rather than silently joined.
- Commentary outside blockquotes is editorial analysis, not Steve's writing.
- Historical punctuation and spelling are retained in quotations. Current preferences in `SKILL.md` govern newly drafted prose.
- Published work may reflect co-author contributions and journal editing. Do not treat every typographical or grammatical choice as an individual preference.
- These are voice references, not verified clinical or methodological guidance.

## 1. Familiar email: acknowledgement, shared ground and reply to points

**Source:** Email to Joe, reproduced from Steve's supplied samples.
**Register:** Familiar professional correspondence.

### Opening and grouped response

> Hi Joe
>
> Thanks for this. The wall of text is fine 🙂.
>
> In fact, I'm trying to get better at writing stuff down. Meetings are helpful, but sometimes we all need the time to think, and we also get the chance to share, and leave a record of those thoughts.
>
> > 1. I think we should do the basics and innovate inside the NHS. We shouldn't outsource the engineering or the innovation and let others profit.
> > 2. I think the NHS should do more commercial (and academic) partnerships but where the NHS is an equal or senior partner, leading on building things we know can bring maximum value into the system
>
> These two are linked. And I think we share the same philosophy. We don't need to do this alone. With the right design, commercial partnerships can be good. The FlowEHR build that Laurence worked on was an example of this going well. If only we'd actually been able to sustain that. I also wonder if there are some _tasks_ that the NHS isn't ready to do yet.

### Closing excerpt

> Again, Joe, I can't thank you enough for bringing a very open and collaborative approach to this. I'm optimistic!
>
> Steve

### What to learn

- The opening responds to something the recipient actually said. It is not an interchangeable pleasantry.
- The nested quotation belongs to the correspondent, not Steve. It demonstrates reply structure, not Steve's sentence-level voice.
- Related points are answered together rather than mechanically one at a time.
- Shared ground precedes qualification.
- A concrete past example supports the argument.
- “I think” and “I also wonder” express judgement and an open question. Removing them would change the stance.
- Fragments, an emoji and an exclamation mark fit this relationship.
- Thanks can appear at both ends when they serve different purposes.

**Do not infer:** Every email needs an emoji, a quoted list or an enthusiastic closing.

## 2. Constructive pushback: turn disagreement into operational tests

**Source:** Email to Chloe, reproduced from Steve's supplied samples.
**Register:** Extended internal email with a worked example.

> Hi Chloe,
>
> Thanks for getting into this. But(!), respectfully, I don't think the current work alone solves the challenges we face. I want to be precise here because the gap between these systems is where our financial risk lies.
>
> * The sheet that you showed me from Stefan is *great*. It shows how to think about the different costing models.
> * The work that you've done with Joe and Julie is *great*. It starts to bring together income and expenditure for CRDM and CRIU.
>
> But... there's a but! I don't think these things can be separate. Whilst they are, we can't make the decisions we need to. I'll try to be concrete and list a few 'tests' we can use to know when we have the solution we need.
>
> 1. Can we say how many days of data engineer time we have allocated vs unallocated in Q2?
> 2. What was the actual profit or loss for the Geosentinel work?
>
> **Example 1: The "Senior Swap" Variance Test**
>
> *Imagine the OMOP course we are doing is a BRC funded grant. Andy is allocated to work on this, but he becomes ill. Stef picks up the work. The course is delivered. Success?*
>
> **The Current View (Siloed):**
> * **Costing Model:** Correctly charged as per NIHR rates.
> * **Finance (Joe/Julie):** All good—money in, salaries out.
>
> **The Reality (Integrated):**
> * **Project Type:** The grant budget is fixed based on Andy's rate.
> * **Variance:** Stef costs significantly more than Andy. Every day Stef worked on this, the department lost money compared to the plan.
> * **Result:** That underwriting is 'invisible' in our current set-up until year-end.
>
> Steve

### What to learn

- The disagreement is explicit and bounded: the work is useful but does not solve the whole problem.
- Praise identifies the contribution rather than merely softening the criticism.
- The reason for precision is stated: financial risk.
- Questions turn an abstract requirement into tests a proposed solution must pass.
- The worked example makes an otherwise hidden consequence visible.
- Numbering supports reference and discussion; it does not imply a sequence.
- Headings and emphasis are useful in an extended email.

**Do not infer:** “But(!)” is a required catchphrase, every disagreement needs parallel blocks, or this historical use of an em dash overrides the current preference against them.

## 3. Strategic briefing: protect relationships without diluting the judgement

**Source:** Document addressed to David, reproduced from Steve's supplied samples.
**Register:** Strategic correspondence with document-level structure.

### Opening excerpt

> Dear David
>
> I would first of all like to thank you for giving us the chance to summarise our concerns with digital innovation at UCLH. I would also like to underline that these observations are not personal criticisms. In fact, I think that the digital, data and EHRS teams have done a remarkable job in a difficult situation. Nonetheless, we think that it is very unlikely that the digital estate will make an important _novel_ contribution to safety or quality over the next 5 years.

### Recommendation excerpt

> Despite this, the _data explorer_ still faces four threats:
>
> 1. The wider governance processes are still not fit for purpose. Contracting, intellectual property discussions, and capability and capacity assessments are extremely difficult to navigate, ad hoc, and extremely slow.
>
>    **Recommendations**
>
>    * **2.1a**: *A task and finish with senior sponsorship from UCLH and UCL, and representation from Information Governance, Commercial and Contracting to build a series of standard enabling templates for the most common use cases.*

### What to learn

- The opening separates criticism of the system from criticism of the people working within it.
- The judgement is clear, but its uncertainty and time horizon remain explicit.
- Acknowledgement precedes the verdict for a reason. The most important sentence need not literally be first.
- The recommendation names sponsorship, representation and a tangible output.
- Recommendation codes make a long document easier to discuss and act on.

**Do not infer:** The opening must be copied at this length, every briefing needs elaborate numbering, or brevity requires deleting relationship-preserving context.

## 4. Sensitive correspondence: state the basis of knowledge and acknowledge the person

**Source:** Complaint-response letter dated 23 June 2022, reproduced from Steve's supplied samples.
**Register:** Formal, emotionally sensitive correspondence.
**Selection:** Limited excerpts; detailed clinical chronology is not reproduced.

### Acknowledgement

> Looking after Mark was made easier for us by your dignity during that time, and we would like to now express our thanks for the courtesy and respect that you brought to all our interactions. He was a young man, and this was a difficult illness.

### Basis of the response

> I will address this from my own recollection and having reviewed the medical notes and discussed with my colleagues.

### Closing

> We are so sorry that Mark's final illness was so cruel, and would like to extend our sympathy.
>
> If you would like to meet to discuss this in person then do please let us know.
>
> Sincerely

### What to learn

- Warmth is specific, but direct expressions of sympathy are also appropriate.
- A short sentence can carry emotional weight without embellishment.
- The basis of the account is made explicit rather than implying perfect personal knowledge.
- The offer to meet is concrete.
- Plain paragraphs keep attention on the person and the response.

**Do not infer:** Sympathy is filler, all external correspondence must use this register, or “Sincerely” is the only acceptable formal closing.

**Privacy:** These excerpts are for private calibration. Do not transfer names or circumstances into new drafts.

## 5. Personal narrative: emotional honesty and dry humour

**Source:** Eulogy, reproduced from Steve's supplied samples.
**Register:** Spoken personal narrative.

### Opening excerpt

> Something like 20 years ago, I gave the best man's speech at Mum's wedding to Keith in this room. I got slightly choked when I did that, and I suspect the same will happen this time. Just warning you.
>
> I'd like to start with a quote from a movie called Interstellar. Mum was not a fan of science fiction, but that's OK. It's my miniature revenge for years of Andrew Lloyd Weber musicals.

### Later interpretive passage

> There's a negative reading of that quote, that sees parenting as being subservient to your kids, as giving up your life for them. I think that's wrong. I'd suggest that being the ghost of your children's future *is being* a parent.

### What to learn

- The opening establishes a shared place and memory rather than announcing a thesis.
- Vulnerability is acknowledged plainly.
- Humour comes from a specific relationship, not an inserted joke.
- “Just warning you” preserves spoken rhythm.
- The later argument is direct but personal: “I think that's wrong.”
- Emphasis identifies the distinction that matters.

**Do not infer:** Personal writing should be front-loaded like a briefing, every emotional passage needs humour, or anecdotes may be invented to reproduce this effect.

## 6. Original research: define the real comparison and preserve uncertainty

**Source:** Harris et al., “Impact on mortality of prompt admission to critical care for deteriorating ward patients: an instrumental variable analysis using critical care bed strain.” *Intensive Care Medicine* (2018).
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-018-5148-2
**Authorship:** Co-authored research paper.
**Register:** Research introduction and discussion.

### Framing the comparison

> Delay can only be defined with hindsight. The bedside choice is to ‘admit now’ or to ‘watch and wait’, not to admit now or deliberately delay. A clinician neither admits the watched patient who improved nor can admit the watched patient who unexpectedly died. The delayed admissions are the subgroup of watchful-waiting controls who survive but continue to deteriorate. A fair comparison requires prospective follow-up of all watchful-waiting controls.

### Interpreting the findings

> There is a large amount of uncertainty in the precise estimate of these effects, but the weight of evidence favours a prompt admission strategy, especially where the bedside assessor recommends critical care.

### Closing implication

> A substantial proportion of patients recommended for critical care are not offered a bed, and this proportion increases when capacity is limited. Although expanding critical care bed numbers may help, supply quickly saturates this expensive resource [32]. Given that the benefit of prompt critical care is unlikely to be equal for all referrals, the challenge now is to better prioritise. Interestingly, this same approach has also been highlighted as crucial to both patients and the public [33].

### What to learn

- The methodological argument begins with the decision available to the clinician.
- A short opening claim is followed by the reasoning needed to justify it.
- Concrete patient trajectories explain the selection problem.
- The distinction between a retrospective category and a prospective decision is the substance, not a rhetorical flourish.
- Uncertainty and the practical interpretation coexist.
- The final paragraph moves from a capacity problem to a prioritisation question without claiming that one intervention solves everything.

**Do not infer:** Direct writing requires categorical conclusions, methodological detail is expendable, or this co-authored text establishes every aspect of Steve's individual style.

## 7. Academic editorial: judge the engineering, not just the score

**Source:** Steve Harris, “I Don't Want My Algorithm to Die in a Paper: Detecting Deteriorating Patients Early.” *American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine* (2021).
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1164/rccm.202102-0459ED
**Authorship:** Sole-authored editorial.
**Register:** Technical commentary with a conversational edge.

### Reframing the reason to care

> But predictive scores with nice acronyms are two-a-penny. So why should we care? Because, reading the report carefully, this is a score that is built with implementation in mind.

### Concrete implementation constraints

> In contrast, acute medicine does not have that luxurious timescale or reliable data source. Ward vital signs change as often as hourly, laboratory values are inconsistently measured, and the technology must update hundreds of patients instantly and simultaneously rather than batch process mammograms overnight. Building a risk score that supports bedside clinical care for hundreds of patients across an entire hospital is as much about engineering as about machine learning. This requires a different set of trade-offs, and it is, therefore, exciting to see such a score performing well.

### Closing excerpt

> I now cannot wait to see the third stage being implemented and these tools returning predictions to the bedside. Work such as this is a stepping stone to true translational data science. We are not short of algorithms, but the majority of these end their lives in an academic report. For machine learning to have its promised impact on health care, we need engineering pipelines, and we need pragmatic algorithms. This paper hints at the former and strongly delivers the latter.

### What to learn

- A rhetorical question changes the criterion by which the work is judged, then receives an immediate answer.
- Colloquial language can coexist with technical precision.
- Operational detail explains why implementation is a different problem from retrospective prediction.
- Enthusiasm is present and earned. A blanket ban would misrepresent the voice.
- The ending distinguishes what the paper suggests from what it actually delivers.
- Repetition of “we need” carries the argument rather than merely filling space.

**Do not infer:** Every editorial needs a provocative opening, enthusiasm is always padding, or a strong closing sentence is inherently artificial.

## 8. Policy commentary: expose the choice, show the example, ask for action

**Source:** Harris, Palmer and Fong, “Trials in pandemics: here we go again?” *British Journal of Anaesthesia*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2020.10.008
**Authorship:** Co-authored correspondence; all authors contributed to the idea and writing.
**Register:** Urgent policy argument grounded in clinical experience.

### The consequential choice

> We face a stark choice in our approach to the management of COVID-19. It is not the choice between drug A and drug B, but whether or not we are prepared to continue to derive policy without robust scientific evidence.

### Concrete account

> Take just one example: the limited question of ventilatory support for severe COVID-19 pneumonia. At University College Hospital London, our first COVID-19 patient arrived on March 6, 2020. Reports from China and Italy indicated that our ICU would be overrun. We contacted colleagues from those countries and designed a new care pathway overnight. We triaged patients after an ‘oxygen challenge’ to either CPAP for those who responded, or invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) for those who did not. In a neighbouring London teaching hospital, a different team made the same phone calls but came to very different conclusions. There, CPAP was largely excluded in favour of early IMV.

### From criticism to a mechanism

> We could and should go further and deploy ‘pandemic data officers’ to the frontline to assist with data collection, minimise logistical burden, and accelerate learning. We could cluster randomise hospitals to strategies that preserve surgical pathways for cancer to a greater or lesser extent. These are pared down solutions for extraordinary times, but could still be implemented for this winter with only modest resource.

### Closing excerpt

> The pandemic is not over, and so the cost of unanswered questions escalates. The sooner we optimise care pathways for COVID-19 patients, the sooner we can redeploy resources to essential non-COVID-19 care. To restore the health service for all, we must immediately learn from the successes of clinical drug trials. We must not be content with retrospective policy review. We must not continue to create policy on the basis of phone calls to friends.

### What to learn

- “Not X, but Y” identifies the actual policy choice. Removing it would weaken the reasoning.
- The local example includes the authors' own untested practice. The criticism is not reserved for others.
- The proposal identifies people, tasks and a study mechanism.
- Urgency is tied to the accumulating cost of uncertainty.
- Repeated sentence openings deliberately strengthen the close.
- The argument ends in an explicit demand, not merely an implied call to action.

**Do not infer:** Every draft should sound urgent, repeated constructions are always undesirable, or the historical claims should be reused as current facts.

## 9. Technical editorial: explain the measure and distinguish its purpose

**Source:** Steve Harris, “Sepsis-3: Syndromes and Empiricism in the Age of Big Data.” *American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine* (2017).
**Authorship:** Sole-authored editorial.
**Register:** Methodological explanation for a clinical audience.

### Explaining discrimination

> Part of the answer is that this comparison is largely based on measures of discrimination wherein the scores for discordant pairs of patients (a survivor and a nonsurvivor) are examined. Counting the number of times the scores correctly order (rank) the risk of the nonsurvivor above that of the survivor and dividing by the number of possible pairs produces the c-index, which is also equivalent to the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) (9).

### Moving to the implication

> So where does this leave us? We certainly should not replace these existing EWS systems with qSOFA, but this was never the intention. But neither should we wish for a Sepsis 3.1 revision with NEWS replacing qSOFA.

### Practical judgement

> We can agree that better scores exist, and where additional data are available to the clinician or the research team, then we should not be satisfied with parsimony. Churpek and colleagues remind us here that we should use all the information available when assessing the patient in front of us (6).

### What to learn

- The statistical quantity is explained through the operation used to calculate it.
- A longer sentence is justified when its components are needed to understand the measure.
- The question marks a transition from explanation to judgement.
- Two conclusions that might seem to follow are explicitly distinguished.
- The closing interpretation returns to the clinical task rather than leaving the discussion at the level of a metric.

**Do not infer:** Technical writing must consist of short sentences, every statistical explanation needs an analogy, or a voice sample substitutes for checking the underlying methodological claim.

## 10. Teaching and professional development: make the workflow tangible

**Source:** “Data Science for Docs.” *Bulletin*, Issue 104, July 2017.
**Authorship:** Co-authored guest editorial with Steve Harris, Ahmed al-Hindawi, Finn Catling, Ed Palmer and Danny Wong listed.
**Source status:** The supplied PDF contains unfinished affiliation placeholders. Treat it as the supplied version, not an independently verified final publication.
**Register:** Explanatory professional writing for clinicians.

### Analogy and reproducibility

> Moreover, most of the work of a practising statistician is in preparing data. This is no different to working in a wet lab where the ‘killer’ experiment can’t be done until you have validated your reagents and calibrated your machines. The preparatory procedures must be documented and reproducible for the final table, figure or statistical test to have meaning.

### A definition followed by its reason

> We believe that Data Science actually summarises a much better approach. Very simply it means documenting your work as code. Why code? Not just because it automates the process, but much more importantly it requires you to be precise about what you have done. We believe that with modern, free, open-source tools this is achievable with a very small amount of training.

### Showing the practical benefit

> We teach the concept of a ‘data pipeline’, so that reproducibility becomes an immediate advantage. A single document, or script, encodes the steps necessary to take data from an online web form into R and out into a report, a simple analysis, or a beautiful data visualisation. When new data is entered, a single keystroke is needed to rerun the entire analysis and update the output. When you come back to your work after a break, the steps required to produce your report are clearly documented. And when you want to share your work, it’s as simple as sending a copy of your code to a collaborator.

### What to learn

- An analogy connects unfamiliar practice to a familiar scientific discipline.
- The definition is simple, but the explanation does not stop at a slogan.
- “Why code?” introduces the reason rather than decorating the paragraph.
- The important benefit is precision and reproducibility, not automation alone.
- Repeated “when” clauses show distinct practical situations.
- This academic-professional register includes contractions and approachable language.
- “Moreover”, contrast and a three-part sequence all do useful work here.

**Do not infer:** Formality requires inflated vocabulary, every transition must be removed, or technical writing should avoid accessible examples.

## Cross-sample calibration

### Patterns supported across the material

- Direct judgements followed by enough explanation to justify them.
- Concrete decisions, constraints, workflows and consequences.
- Clear distinctions between superficially similar questions.
- Explicit ownership of opinion and uncertainty.
- Criticism that identifies what works as well as what remains unresolved.
- Structure proportional to the complexity of the task.
- Warmth grounded in the relationship or circumstances.
- Conclusions that state what follows, rather than merely repeating what came before.

### Features that vary by register

- Length of the opening acknowledgement.
- Contractions, fragments and humour.
- Headings, lists and emphasis.
- Rhetorical questions and repetition.
- Strength of the closing appeal.
- Use of personal narrative.

### Do not turn these samples into rigid prohibitions

The evidence does not support blanket bans on enthusiasm, rhetorical questions, three-part lists, contrast, repetition, longer sentences or strong endings.

Equally, an occurrence in a sample does not make a feature mandatory. Preserve the reasoning and the relationship, not a collection of surface mannerisms.
